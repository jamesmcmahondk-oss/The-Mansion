Game Design Overview

The Mansion

A gothic point-and-click adventure about memory, identity, grief, and the difficult business of letting go.

A dead player awakens at the gates of a Mansion that feels familiar. Inside and around it live strange residents who are not puzzles disguised as people, but people whose unresolved struggles have become puzzles.

Creative North Star

What the game is

The Mansion is a narrative-driven gothic adventure built in C# and WPF. It combines classic point-and-click exploration with light role-playing statistics, inventory puzzles, branching dialogue, persistent world states, character choices, and darkly comic storytelling.

The Mansion is not primarily about discovering why a house is haunted. It is about discovering why the player belongs there.

Gothic, not grim

Rain, candlelight, iron gates, wet stone, impossible rooms and old grief create the atmosphere. Humour keeps the world human and prevents the darkness from becoming monotonous.

Characters before lore

The residents are eccentric, funny and occasionally dangerous, but each must feel like a real person with needs, contradictions and history.

Mystery through familiarity

The player is not frightened because the Mansion is completely alien. The Mansion is disturbing because pieces of it feel remembered.

Choices with emotional weight

Many residents may be helped, harmed, killed, ignored or released. The meaningful question is not simply whether the player solved the puzzle, but what the player chose to do with another person's pain.

Narrative Premise

The player and the Mansion

The player arrives at the gates with no memory of the journey. The Mansion once contained ordinary life: servants crossing the rear yard, vegetables in the kitchen garden, candles in the Glasshouse, music through open windows. Nobody remembers exactly when that life stopped.

The Mansion is a purgatory-like place populated by residents who embody psychological, emotional and existential struggles. The player is also dead, but the game should reveal that slowly through environmental inconsistencies, dialogue, Mementos and the player's own inability to remember.

The central dramatic progression is:

Enter→Explore→Remember→Choose→Accept or resist

Core questions

  • Why does the Mansion feel familiar?
  • Why can the residents not move on?
  • What are the Mementos restoring?
  • What did the player lose, deny or refuse to accept?
  • Does helping others change the player?
  • Is escape the same thing as resolution?
Player Motivation

Objectives

Immediate objective

Find a way through the gates, cross the grounds and gain entry to the Mansion.

Exploration objective

Map the Mansion and estate, unlock shortcuts, investigate inaccessible spaces and understand how the exterior and interior connect.

Character objective

Meet the residents, learn what keeps each one trapped and decide whether to help, exploit, confront, avoid or destroy them.

Memory objective

Recover Mementos that provoke fragments of the player's forgotten life and gradually reveal the player's connection to the Mansion.

Personal objective

Discover what the player refuses to accept and determine whether the player can change rather than merely escape.

End-state objective

Reach an ending shaped by the player's treatment of the residents, recovered memories, unresolved consequences and final response to the truth.

Puzzle Philosophy

How puzzles should work

Stats create shortcuts

A strong Wits score may reconstruct a worn inscription immediately. A failed roll must not permanently block the quest.

Items provide reliable solutions

Charcoal and paper can produce a guaranteed gravestone rubbing. The player who explores carefully always retains a route forward.

World logic over key logic

The Kitchen Door is bolted from inside and becomes a shortcut later. It does not demand another conveniently placed key.

Behaviour can be the solution

A statue may release a sword only when the player approaches unarmed. The solution is understanding the instruction, not locating a matching token.

Failure should teach

The rose bush hurts once, then the player refuses to repeat the mistake. Garden Shears provide the proper solution and yield a Red Rose.

Not every object is a puzzle

The lone black sock in the Rear Yard is simply damp. It remains damp. Its refusal to become important is part of the joke.

The best puzzles reveal character, geography or theme while they are being solved.
World Structure

The estate as a coherent place

The Mansion should feel physically navigable rather than like a list of disconnected backgrounds. Exterior routes form a ring, while later shortcuts connect the grounds to the interior and underground spaces.

Front Gate→Courtyard→Mansion Right→Rear Yard→Kitchen Garden→Glasshouse / Garden→Courtyard

Front Gate

The opening threshold. One gate pillar still holds a sad gargoyle. The opposite pillar is empty because the other gargoyle simply left.

Courtyard

The grand public face of the estate, with the fountain, formal approach and path toward the front door.

Mansion Right

The quieter servants' side, containing the stone bench, rose bush, service entrance and Ebria's hidden first encounter.

Rear Yard

A wet working courtyard with Tool Shed, Kitchen Door, Cellar Doors, hand pump, trough, drain and the canonical damp sock.

Kitchen Garden

The transition from stone to earth to glass: vegetable beds, herbs, cold frames, espalier trees and a path toward the Glasshouse.

Glasshouse

A warm, humid and inexplicably maintained structure filled with tropical plants and candles. Its beauty should be more unsettling than overt horror.

Well and Graveyard

Branches from the exterior loop containing deeper environmental puzzles, a mysterious mirror, worn inscriptions and the first major Memento opportunity.

Interior shortcuts

The Rear Yard Kitchen Door is opened from inside. The Cellar Doors may later connect to underground areas. Unlocking routes changes the map permanently.

Character Design

Residents of the Mansion

The cast should first appear entertaining, strange or threatening. Their deeper emotional truth is revealed gradually, usually after the player has already formed an attachment.

Ebria

A sharp, funny and occasionally cruel fairy found drinking beneath a stone bench. Alcohol is armour, not personality. Her story concerns grief, identity and life after losing a mirror twin. Early dialogue protects the words sister, twin, mirror, photograph and violet ribbon.

Sad Gargoyle

An ancient guardian still sitting on a gate pillar because duty feels stronger than mortar. The other gargoyle simply left, turning the empty pillar into both a joke and a persuasive argument for choice.

King Hautain

A tiny frog monarch with a crown, territory and uncompromising opinions about sovereignty. The character is ridiculous, but the world treats the title with just enough seriousness to preserve dignity.

Quazimodo

Butler, doorman and cleaner of visitors who mistake those duties for weakness. Dry, patient and physically formidable, with more control over Mansion access than first impressions suggest.

The wider cast

Severin, Pip, Velvet, Hollis, Grell, Magnus and the other residents carry distinct struggles, histories and possible resolutions. No character should exist merely as a lore dispenser or quest vending machine.

The player

The central unresolved resident. The player can diagnose everyone else while avoiding the same scrutiny. Mementos and choices gradually close that escape route.

Aesthetic and Voice

Style, tone and presentation

Visual language

Realistic cinematic Gothic environments with fixed-camera compositions, rain-slick stone, dark timber, rusted iron, moss, ivy, wet earth and restrained amber candlelight.

Scenes must remain functional game spaces. Doors look clickable, paths remain readable, foregrounds accommodate sprites and UI, and the visual hierarchy guides movement without signs or arrows.

Writing voice

Dry, observant and emotionally restrained. Characters deflect sincerity with wit. The game allows truth to appear briefly before someone covers it with a joke.

Humour comes from character logic, not random gags. The gargoyle treats leaving as an administrative crisis. Hautain treats a fallen fairy as a military invasion. Ebria treats kindness as an inconvenience.

Tone rule: Let comedy make the player care before the story explains why the behaviour was never entirely funny.

Creative Lineage

Inspirations and reference points

The game draws from several traditions without attempting to imitate any one title directly.

Classic point-and-click adventures

Readable hotspots, inventory reasoning, dialogue-led discovery, authored scenes and the pleasure of connecting distant details.

Gothic literature and architecture

Secrets embedded in buildings, unreliable memory, decaying estates, thresholds, family histories, candlelight, rain and the persistence of the past.

Psychological purgatory stories

A place shaped by unresolved lives, where escape depends on recognition, acceptance or transformation rather than simply opening the correct door.

Dark comedy

Absurdity beside grief. The strange is treated seriously enough to become funny, and the joke often hides a wound.

Role-playing games

Attributes, conditional dialogue, alternate solutions, equipment states and player-defined moral outcomes without turning the experience into a combat-heavy RPG.

Theatre and chamber drama

A contained location, recurring cast, strong entrances, evolving relationships and conversations in which what is avoided matters as much as what is spoken.

Head Writer's Rules

Design principles to protect

  1. People first. Every resident should remain a person, even when the person is a fairy, vampire, frog monarch, gargoyle or ghost.
  2. Do not explain mysteries too early. Use cracks, slips, objects and reactions before exposition.
  3. Preserve the mundane. Tools, cabbages, drains, socks and delivery doors make the supernatural believable.
  4. Let locations connect logically. Rear Yard leads to Kitchen Garden, Kitchen Garden leads to Glasshouse, and doors opened from inside become shortcuts.
  5. Do not confuse obscurity with depth. Clues may be subtle, but the world should remain internally coherent.
  6. Failure must create information. Bad rolls and wrong interactions should teach, redirect or entertain instead of merely blocking progress.
  7. Avoid arbitrary key chains. Prefer bolts, relationships, state changes, behaviour, observation and environmental cause-and-effect.
  8. Use humour as camouflage. A funny line should often become sadder after the player learns the truth.
  9. Choices must reveal the player. The important consequence is not only what happened, but what the decision says about the protagonist.
  10. The final mystery is personal. The Mansion is not merely waiting to be understood. The player is.
People lived here. Something ended, but not everything moved on.
